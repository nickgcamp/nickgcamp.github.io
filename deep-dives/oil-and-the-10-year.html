Oil, the 10-year, and the day the most reliable trade of 2026 stopped working

The oil–yield link hasn't disappeared. It switches on and off depending on what the bond market fears most.

By Nick CampuzanoNov 9, 2026Markets10 min read

Key takeaway

Oil and the 10-year usually move together. When they split up, it tells you what the bond market is most scared of that day.

In September oil and the 10-year were moving together more tightly than they had in 35 years, by Cboe's measure. Then in October it broke. Oil kept climbing while the 10-year yield dropped about 0.4 percentage points from its peak, because investors got more worried about growth (an AI-led stock selloff, riskier companies paying more to borrow) than about inflation.

The short version

  1. Oil and yields move together when inflation and the Fed are the big worry. They split up when the worry turns to growth, government debt, or news that only hits oil. In 2026 they went from moving almost in lockstep to not moving together at all within weeks. By early November they were moving in opposite directions for the first time since the Iran conflict began.
  2. Even in lockstep, oil pushed yields up by pushing the Fed, not by changing what people expect inflation to be years from now. The market's 10-year inflation guess barely moved, staying between 2.2% and 2.5% all year. Meanwhile the inflation-adjusted 10-year yield rose from about 1.7% to as high as about 2.9%. So once oil stopped mattering to the Fed, the link snapped fast.
  3. Never read the 10-year alone. Split each move into two parts: how much is inflation worry, and how much is the Fed and everything else. Then check what stocks and riskier bonds are doing.

1. The two numbers, taken apart

Two numbers every morning: the 10-year Treasury yield (about 4.86% at the November 6, 2026 close) and WTI, the main US oil price (about $88–91 a barrel lately). The 10-year is the baseline rate almost everything else gets priced off, and oil is the most important commodity price. How tightly the two move together changes over time, and that tells you what the market is afraid of.

A 10-year Treasury note is a loan to the US government for 10 years. Its yield is three things stacked: where the Fed is heading, how much inflation people expect, and a little extra pay for locking your money up for 10 years.

  • The Fed path. Where the Fed sets short-term rates and where people think it's heading. The 2-year yield is the best quick read.
  • Expected inflation. Traders call it the breakeven. It's the gap between a normal Treasury and one that's protected against inflation (TIPS).
  • Term premium. Extra pay for locking money up for 10 years instead of rolling over short-term loans. It comes from models, and they disagree a little.

Why this matters: oil can only move the 10-year by moving one of these three parts. Most people assume oil works through expected inflation. In 2026 it mostly worked through the Fed, and when that stopped, the link fell apart.

What moves the price of oil

  • OPEC+ supply policy. In 2025 OPEC+ chased market share, raising targets by 411,000 barrels per day in each of May, June and July 2025. The IEA said the surprise fueled a “downward spiral” in prices.
  • US shale. The Dallas Fed's survey of oil companies put the price a new well needs to make money at $65 a barrel in early 2025 and $66 in early 2026.
  • Demand, geopolitics and the dollar. 2026 is the textbook case: the US-Israel war with Iran and disruption of the Strait of Hormuz.

The key point: if oil rises because people are buying more, that's good news for growth and pushes up inflation, so yields rise. If it rises because supply got cut off, like in a war, it works like a tax on consumers. Prices go up first, and the economy slows later.

2. Why oil and yields usually move together

  1. Inflation expectations. Bruegel estimated that before 2016 a 10% rise in oil lifted the inflation people expect over the next 10 years by about 0.1 percentage points. The Boston Fed estimated in 2026 that a 33% jump in oil raises next year's inflation by about 1.5 points, down from about 2.2 before the mid-1980s.
  2. The growth signal. The late-2016 “reflation trade” (betting the economy would heat up): OPEC's first cut since 2008 sent Brent, the global oil price, to a 16-month high and the 10-year to its highest since July 2015.
  3. The Fed's reaction. The biggest reason in 2026. The Fed raised rates a quarter point on September 16, 2026, to 3.75–4.00%, its first hike since July 2023, pointing to energy prices pushing inflation up.
  4. Inflation bets. The FRED Blog found oil and the market's 5-year inflation guess moved closely together from 2011 to March 2019 (a 0.65 correlation), and even more closely in 2015–2019 (0.85).
  5. Petrodollar recycling. Oil countries parking their dollars in Treasuries. Mostly history now. As Brad Setser put it in April 2026: “The glory days of the petrodollar are over.”

A quick warning on how this gets measured. Compare yearly averages of the two and you get nonsense (one op-ed got −0.70 over 1987–2025), because both drift over the decades. People who do this for a living compare daily or weekly changes over a moving window, and the window matters: BMO's one-month window showed 0.96 while Cboe's three-month window topped out around 0.65.

3. A history of on and off

The two have switched on and off many times. When oil moves because demand changes, they move together. When the Fed, government debt or a supply story is in charge, they pull apart.

4. The 2025–2026 story in four acts

Act I, 2025: the first split

Oil fell because there was too much of it: US oil dropped 20% in 2025, its biggest yearly drop since 2020. Yields stayed high anyway. The Fed cut three times, yet the 10-year averaged about 4.29%, held up by worries about government debt and a term premium RSM put at 0.8 points, up nearly 0.7 since the April 2025 tariff announcement. The deficit was $1.775 trillion in FY2025 and $1.99 trillion in FY2026.

Act II, March to September 2026: back in lockstep

The Iran war gave US oil its biggest weekly gain since it started trading in 1983, more than 35%, with a close as high as $114.58 on April 7. Inflation climbed from 2.4% in February to 3.7% in September, the Fed hiked, and the 10-year peaked at a 5.31% close on October 5. In March, the S&P 500 fell 5.13% and 10-year bonds lost about 1.97%, so bonds didn't cushion the fall the way they're supposed to.

Act III, October 2026: the break

October 22 gave it away. Oil rose 4% and yields fell anyway, because people were more worried about stocks and the economy than about inflation.

“Oil and treasury yields had this amazing correlation and in October it just stopped... the geopolitical risk is secondary to AI risk.”Jeffrey Rosenberg, BlackRock

Stephane Deo of Eleva Capital summed up the new story: higher oil now means growth fears, and growth fears mean lower yields.

Act IV, November 2026: oil collapses, yields drift

Brent fell 7.6% on November 2 on peace hopes and settled at $98.32 on November 6. A blowout October jobs report (+165,000 vs. about 60,000 expected) pushed the 10-year back to about 4.86%, and traders put the odds of a December rate hike at 76.8% (CME FedWatch). Oil fell about 13–16% in a week and the 10-year barely moved. The 10-year is now tied to the Fed and the term premium, not oil.

5. Why the link broke

  1. Growth fear overtook inflation fear. The main reason, and nearly everyone agrees on it. “Bonds do NOT hedge inflation. They hedge the slowdown that bad inflation eventually causes.” (Lance Roberts)
  2. The Fed link was maxed out. Fed research (Li and Yoldas, October 2026) finds that since 2021, 10-year yields react about three times as much to inflation surprises as in 2000–2019.
  3. Crowded bets. Lots of traders had bet against Treasuries going into October. Real, but probably secondary.
  4. Government borrowing and the term premium. These set the overall level; oil and growth drive the day-to-day wiggles.
  5. The US now exports more oil than it imports. So higher oil may now help the US economy. The evidence is mixed.

Putting it together: 2025 was a lasting split, spring and summer 2026 were a temporary return to lockstep through the Fed, and October 2026 flipped the switch to growth fears. The link isn't broken; the switch is set to “growth” for now.

6. What it did to other markets

Inside stocks, October split the market: the average S&P 500 stock rose 4.95% (equal-weight) while the regular, size-weighted index (SPY) lost 0.91%, as energy gained about 10% over three weeks and chip stocks fell about 16%.

7. How to read it every morning

The one habit that matters most: whenever the 10-year makes a big move, split it in two.

Change in the 10-year = change in expected inflation + change in the inflation-adjusted yield (the Fed and everything else)

Then read oil and the 10-year together:
