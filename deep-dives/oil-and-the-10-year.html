Oil, the 10-year, and the day the most reliable trade of 2026 stopped working

The oil–yield link hasn't disappeared. It switches on and off depending on what the bond market fears most.

By Nick CampuzanoNov 9, 2026Markets10 min read

Key takeaway

WTI and the 10-year are not two readings of one force. They are two outputs of a system with several inputs. Whether they move together or apart tells you which input is in charge that day.

In September 2026 the link ran at a 35-year high, with Cboe measuring the 3-month rolling correlation between the 10-year yield and WTI at 65%. Then it broke in October: crude kept climbing while the 10-year Treasury yield fell about 40 basis points from its peak, because investors started worrying more about growth (an AI-led equity selloff, widening credit spreads) than about inflation.

The short version

  1. Oil and yields co-move when inflation and Fed fears dominate, and decouple when growth fears, fiscal and term-premium forces, or oil-specific supply news take over. In 2026 the correlation went from extreme to broken within weeks. By early November it had turned negative for the first time since the Iran conflict began.
  2. Even during the lockstep, oil moved yields through the Fed and real rates, not long-run inflation expectations. The 10-year breakeven sat in a narrow 2.2–2.5% band all year, while the 10-year TIPS real yield rose from about 1.7% to as high as about 2.9%. So when oil stopped mattering to the Fed path, the link snapped quickly.
  3. Never read the 10-year alone. Split each move into breakevens (inflation compensation) and real yields (Fed path plus term premium), and check stocks and credit spreads.

1. The two numbers, taken apart

Two numbers every morning: the 10-year Treasury yield (about 4.86% at the November 6, 2026 close) and WTI crude (about $88–91 a barrel in recent sessions). The 10-year is the benchmark discount rate for nearly every asset in the world, and oil is the most important commodity price. Their coupling changes over time, and the strength of that coupling tells you what the market is afraid of.

A 10-year Treasury note is a loan to the US government for 10 years. Because its price is set in a market, its yield is an aggregate forecast plus a risk charge:

10-year yield ≈ expected short-term real rates (the Fed path) + expected inflation (breakevens) + term premium

  • The Fed path. Where the Fed sets its policy rate and where markets think it's heading. The 2-year yield is the best single read.
  • Breakevens. The nominal yield minus the TIPS (real) yield. On FRED: DGS10, DFII10, T10YIE.
  • Term premium. Extra pay for locking money up for 10 years instead of rolling short bills. It comes from models, and they disagree a little.

Why this matters: oil can only move the 10-year by moving one of these three parts. Most people assume oil works through inflation expectations. In 2026 oil mostly worked through the Fed path, and when that channel switched off, the coupling collapsed.

What drives WTI

  • OPEC+ supply policy. In 2025 OPEC+ chased market share, raising targets by 411,000 barrels per day in each of May, June and July 2025. The IEA said the surprise fueled a “downward spiral” in prices.
  • US shale. The Dallas Fed Energy Survey put the average break-even for new wells at $65 a barrel in Q1 2025 and $66 in Q1 2026.
  • Demand, geopolitics and the dollar. 2026 is the textbook case: the US-Israel war with Iran and disruption of the Strait of Hormuz.

The key point: a demand-driven rally is good news for growth and inflation, so yields rise. A supply-driven rally, like a war, is a tax on consumers: inflationary at first, contractionary later.

2. Why oil and yields usually move together

  1. Inflation expectations. Bruegel estimated that before 2016 a 10% rise in oil lifted US 10-year inflation expectations by about 10 bp. The Boston Fed estimated in 2026 that a 33% oil shock raises year-ahead PCE inflation by about 1.5 points, down from about 2.2 before the mid-1980s.
  2. The growth signal. The late-2016 “reflation trade”: OPEC's first cut since 2008 sent Brent to a 16-month high and the 10-year to its highest since July 2015.
  3. The Fed's reaction. The dominant channel in 2026. The Fed raised rates 25 bp on September 16, 2026, to 3.75–4.00%, its first hike since July 2023, citing energy-driven inflation.
  4. Breakevens. The FRED Blog found a 0.65 correlation between WTI and the 5-year breakeven from 2011 to March 2019, rising to 0.85 for 2015–2019.
  5. Petrodollar recycling. Mostly history now. As Brad Setser put it in April 2026: “The glory days of the petrodollar are over.”

One caution on method. Correlating yearly averages of the two levels gives nonsense (one op-ed computed −0.70 over 1987–2025) because both series trend. Practitioners correlate daily or weekly changes over rolling windows, and the window matters: BMO's 1-month window showed 0.96 while Cboe's 3-month window peaked around 0.65.

3. A history of on and off

The coupling has switched regimes many times. Oil-demand shocks tie the two together; Fed, fiscal and supply stories pull them apart.

4. The 2025–2026 story in four acts

Act I, 2025: the first decoupling

Oil fell on a supply glut: WTI dropped 20% in 2025, its sharpest annual decline since 2020. Yields stayed high anyway. The Fed cut three times, yet the 10-year averaged about 4.29%, held up by fiscal worries and a term premium RSM put at 80 bp, up nearly 70 bp since the April 2025 tariff announcement. The deficit was $1.775 trillion in FY2025 and $1.99 trillion in FY2026.

Act II, March to September 2026: the great re-coupling

The Iran war gave WTI its biggest weekly gain since the contract began in 1983, more than 35%, with a close as high as $114.58 on April 7. Headline CPI climbed from 2.4% in February to 3.7% in September, the Fed hiked, and the 10-year peaked at a 5.31% close on October 5. In March, the S&P 500 fell 5.13% and 10-year bonds lost about 1.97%: bonds failed as a hedge.

Act III, October 2026: the break

October 22 was the tell. The S&P 500 fell 1.2% in an AI-led tech selloff, high-yield spreads widened to 3.40%, and about 70% of the yield drop came from real yields: a growth-scare signature.

“Oil and treasury yields had this amazing correlation and in October it just stopped... the geopolitical risk is secondary to AI risk.”Jeffrey Rosenberg, BlackRock

Stephane Deo of Eleva Capital summed up the new story: higher oil now means growth fears, and growth fears mean lower real yields.

Act IV, November 2026: oil collapses, yields drift

Brent fell 7.6% on November 2 on peace hopes and settled at $98.32 on November 6. A blowout October payrolls report (+165,000 vs. about 60,000 expected) pushed the 10-year back to about 4.86%, and CME FedWatch put December hike odds at 76.8%. Oil fell about 13–16% in a week and the 10-year fell only a few basis points. The 10-year is now anchored by the Fed and term premium, not oil.

5. Why the link broke

  1. Growth fear overtook inflation fear. The dominant, near-consensus reason. “Bonds do NOT hedge inflation. They hedge the slowdown that bad inflation eventually causes.” (Lance Roberts)
  2. The Fed channel saturated. Fed research (Li and Yoldas, October 2026) finds that since 2021, 10-year yields respond about three times as much to inflation surprises as in 2000–2019.
  3. Positioning. Speculators were heavily short Treasuries going into October. Real, but probably secondary.
  4. Fiscal supply and term premium. These set the level; oil and growth drive the wiggles.
  5. The US is a net petroleum exporter. Higher oil may now boost US activity. The evidence is mixed.

Putting it together: 2025 was a structural decoupling, Q2 and Q3 2026 were a cyclical re-coupling through the Fed, and October 2026 was a regime flip to growth fears. The coupling isn't broken; the switch is set to “growth” for now.

6. What it did to other markets

Inside stocks, October split the market: the equal-weight S&P 500 rose 4.95% while cap-weighted SPY lost 0.91%, as energy gained about 10% over three weeks and semiconductors fell about 16%.

7. How to read it every morning

The one habit that matters most: for every notable 10-year move, split it in two.

Δ 10-year = Δ breakeven (inflation channel) + Δ real yield (Fed path and term premium)

Then read oil and the 10-year together:

Oil ↑ · 10-year ↑Inflation or Fed scareOr reflation, if stocks also rise.
Oil ↑ · 10-year ↓Supply shock read as bad for growthOctober 2026.
Oil ↓ · 10-year ↑Fiscal worries, term premium or a hawkish FedYields rise despite cheap oil.
Oil ↓ · 10-year ↓Demand collapse or de-escalationBoth fall together.
Oil big ↓ · 10-year small ↓Oil is no longer the driverThe first week of November 2026.
